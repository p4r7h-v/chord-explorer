# Chord progression research library

Researched 2026-09-14. A curated starting set for Chord Explorer, not a ranking or an exhaustive inventory of music. Scope: tonal Western pop, rock, functional harmony, and proposed electronic/cinematic adaptations.

**32 audition candidates: 18 documented patterns or interpretations, 12 editorial variations, and 2 user references.** Related rotations remain grouped as families. No claim that all entries are equally common or proven effective for EDM trap.

## Reading the catalog

All numerals here use **major-referenced root positions**: bIII is three semitones above the tonic, bVI eight, bVII ten, regardless of mode. Uppercase means major; lowercase minor; ° diminished. In E minor, i–bIII–bVII–iv means Em–G–D–Am. The user's screenshot convention omits these flats in minor; its i–III–VII–iv is the same progression.

Every mood tag below is an **editorial audition suggestion**, not an experimentally established emotion attached to that sequence. Names such as “Open horizon” are interface labels invented for this catalog, not historical names. Source links establish the harmonic patterns; they do not endorse our mood labels.

## What the emotion research actually supports

Cheung and colleagues (2019) found an interaction between expectation uncertainty and harmonic surprise in musical pleasure ratings. Their behavioral experiment used 39 participants and 30 progressions selected from a historical Billboard corpus. It did not establish an emotion lookup table for all chord progressions or a contemporary EDM preference ranking. [Primary paper](https://www.marcus-pearce.com/assets/papers/CheungEtAl2019.pdf).

Product implication: let a listener tag what they feel, compare nearby alternatives, and retain their preferences. Treat words like epic, sad, hopeful, tense, and dreamy as overlapping discovery filters. Do not display invented percentages or emotional certainty scores.

## Design consequences

- Start with 12 recommendations and an “All 32” library. Group rotations and variants under their families.
- One key auditions an entire loop. Next/previous changes the candidate at a musical boundary. Compare with the same tonic, tempo, sound, loudness, chord duration, and voicing policy.
- Offer a consistent default of four beats per chord as an audition setting, not a claim about the source's rhythm. Preserve intentional repeated chords such as V05.
- Let users pin A/B candidates, star favorites, and add personal mood tags. Keep a “My references” collection for the nine source examples.
- After choosing a promising loop, reveal variations: rotate start, change one chord, invert, spread, add sevenths, or change bass. Explain the changed notes on the piano.
- Store each chord's root as a semitone offset plus explicit quality. Store bass offset separately when needed. A scale-degree index alone cannot represent major V in minor, borrowed iv, bII, or chromatic bvi.
- Store stable ID, family, tonic/mode interpretation, source URL, evidence class, original notation, normalized steps, and editorial tags. Mood labels must not masquerade as sourced metadata.
- A major/minor toggle should distinguish transposing the same pattern from reharmonizing its qualities. Reharmonization changes the progression; it is not just another key.

## Remaining coverage gaps

This is a useful first research pass, not a universal superset. Add independently verified contemporary EDM examples, jazz-specific minor cadences, blues with actual bar structure, chromatic film-score studies, and more non-Western/context-specific evidence before claiming those areas are covered. The immediate priority is auditioning this set and learning which candidates the user actually likes.
